# Your Kid Doesn't Have to Be Hacked for Your Family to Be Compromised

*One reused password. One forgotten account. One connected device. Sometimes that's all it takes.*

We protect our homes.

We lock the doors. We check the windows. We teach our kids not to open the door for strangers.

But when was the last time you checked the **digital doors** your family uses every day?

A recent report from Cybernews revisited a disturbing case involving a 13-year-old boy whose family's Ring cameras were accessed by an attacker. The cameras became a way for a stranger to speak to the child inside his own home.

The part that caught my attention wasn't some highly sophisticated attack.

It was a reused password.

According to Cybernews, the username and password used to access the Ring account had previously been stolen from an unrelated website. The family had reused those credentials, and that was enough to turn a completely separate breach into a problem inside their home.

[Read the full Cybernews report: **How one person's personal information can put the whole family at risk**](https://cybernews.com/security/how-one-persons-information-can-put-the-family-at-risk/)

And this is where I want parents to stop and think for a minute.

## Your child's account is part of your family's security

We tend to think about cybersecurity as something that happens to **us**.

My email.

My bank account.

My phone.

My work laptop.

But families don't live in separate digital bubbles.

Your child's gaming account may connect to your home Wi-Fi.

Their tablet may have access to the family network.

Their email address may be used to reset other accounts.

A smart TV, gaming console, doorbell camera, phone, laptop, or tablet may all live on the same network.

And somewhere in that chain could be an account that hasn't been updated in years, a password that's been reused too many times, or an old device nobody remembers is still connected.

That's the weak link.

And the weak link doesn't have to be the least technical person in the family.

It could simply be the person who hasn't thought about their digital security in a while.

## The scary part isn't the hacker

I don't want you to read a story like this and walk away thinking:

**"I need to become a cybersecurity expert."**

You don't.

You need to start thinking about your household the same way you think about your physical home.

You wouldn't give the same key to every door and assume you're safe because the front door is locked.

So why do we use the same password everywhere?

You wouldn't leave every window unlocked because you have an alarm system.

So why rely on one password when your important accounts can use multi-factor authentication?

And you wouldn't knowingly leave an old door standing open.

So why keep devices, apps, and accounts connected that nobody uses anymore?

Cybersecurity at home isn't about building a fortress.

It's about closing the doors you don't need open.

## Kids can be part of the solution

This is something I talk about a lot at DadCyberTips.

We have to stop treating cybersecurity as something adults handle **for** kids.

Kids need to understand it too.

Not because we expect a nine-year-old to become a security engineer.

Because they're already part of the digital household.

Start with simple conversations:

**"Do you use that password anywhere else?"**

**"Do you know what information that game is asking you for?"**

**"Would you tell me if somebody online asked for your password?"**

**"What would you do if you clicked something that looked weird?"**

And most importantly:

**"You will never be in trouble for telling me something went wrong."**

That last one matters.

A child who is afraid of getting in trouble may hide a compromised account, a strange message, or a mistake.

A child who knows they can come to you gives your family something technology can't provide:

**an early warning system.**

## And don't forget Grandma and Grandpa

The same family-security principle applies at the other end of the age spectrum.

Older relatives are frequently targeted by phishing, impersonation, and scam calls. Their accounts may also be connected to family email, shared photos, financial information, or other services.

The answer isn't to tell Grandma she can't use the internet anymore.

It's to help her use it safely.

Sit down together.

Look at the accounts she uses.

Make sure important passwords aren't being reused.

Turn on MFA where possible.

Update the phone.

Remove apps she doesn't use.

And establish one simple family rule:

**If something feels urgent, scary, or unusual, call me before you act.**

That single conversation can be worth more than another security app.

## Your family digital checkup

If you want to do something useful today, don't try to fix everything.

Take 20 minutes.

### 1. Find your important accounts

Start with:

- Primary email
- Banking
- Apple or Google account
- Social media
- Gaming accounts
- Cloud storage
- Smart-home accounts
- Family streaming accounts

### 2. Look for reused passwords

If the same password is protecting multiple accounts, start changing them.

Your important accounts should have unique passwords.

A password manager can make this much easier.

### 3. Turn on MFA

Start with email, banking, cloud storage, social media, and any account that contains sensitive information.

Your email deserves special attention because it can often be used to reset everything else.

### 4. Check the devices connected to your Wi-Fi

Look at your router's connected-device list.

You may find phones, TVs, gaming consoles, cameras, tablets, printers, and other devices you forgot were connected.

If you don't recognize something, investigate it.

### 5. Update the things you forgot about

Phones.

Tablets.

Gaming consoles.

Routers.

Smart TVs.

Cameras.

Old laptops.

Those devices don't stop being part of your security just because you stopped thinking about them.

### 6. Have the conversation

Ask every person in your house:

**"If something happened online and you weren't sure what to do, would you feel comfortable telling me?"**

If the answer isn't immediately yes, that's the first thing I'd work on.

## The lesson isn't "be scared"

Stories like this can make parents want to lock everything down.

I understand that.

But that's not the goal.

The goal isn't to make your kids afraid of technology.

It isn't to make grandparents afraid to answer their phones.

And it isn't to turn your house into a digital bunker.

The goal is **awareness**.

Know what is connected.

Know what information you're sharing.

Know which accounts matter most.

Know what your family should do when something doesn't look right.

And make sure everyone knows that mistakes can be reported without fear.

Because eventually, somebody in your family will click something they shouldn't.

Somebody will get a suspicious message.

Somebody will forget a password.

Somebody will download the wrong app.

That's life.

The difference between a small mistake and a family-wide problem is often how quickly someone recognizes it and asks for help.

## There is no God Mode for your family

I grew up playing games like **DOOM** and **StarCraft**.

Back then, finding the right cheat code or hack could completely change the game.

Suddenly you had abilities you weren't supposed to have.

You felt almost unstoppable.

In DOOM, there was even **God Mode**.

Real life doesn't work that way.

There isn't a cheat code that makes your family impossible to compromise.

But there is something better than pretending you're invincible:

**knowing what to look for.**

That's what DadCyberTips is about.

Not fear.

Not technical jargon.

Not expecting parents, kids, or grandparents to become cybersecurity experts.

Just giving families the knowledge to recognize the digital doors around them—and helping them learn how to keep those doors closed when they should be.

**Security starts at home.**

And sometimes, the best thing you can do today is simply start the conversation.
