# AI Scams Are Getting Smarter. Your Family Can Get Smarter, Too.

*You don't have to be bad with technology to fall for a scam. You just have to be human.*

Picture this.

You get a text from your bank. It says there's a problem with your account and asks you to verify a recent transaction.

Or your parent gets a call from someone who sounds exactly like their grandchild, saying they need money urgently.

Maybe your teenager receives an email about a job they applied for. The company name is right. The position is right. Even the details sound familiar.

Would you recognize the scam?

Here's the uncomfortable truth: **the old signs of a scam aren't always reliable anymore.**

Spelling mistakes, awkward messages, and suspicious-looking emails can still be warning signs. But artificial intelligence is helping scammers create messages that sound natural, personal, and convincing.

And that changes how we need to teach our families to stay safe online.

## The problem isn't that people are gullible

I want to make something clear right away.

Falling for a scam doesn't mean you're stupid.

Scammers understand something about people: we respond to emotion.

We want to protect our children. We want to help our parents. We want to keep our money safe. We don't want to miss a job opportunity or have our bank account locked.

Scammers use those instincts against us.

AI can help them write more convincing messages, tailor fake communications to a particular person, and imitate the tone of a legitimate business or familiar contact.

The result? A message can look perfectly reasonable while the request behind it is anything but.

That's why the answer isn't simply to tell everyone to “look for bad grammar.”

**The better skill is learning to question the request—not just the message.**

## Three situations worth thinking about

The financial publication Stacking Paper explored this problem with examples involving bank alerts, peer-to-peer payments, and job application emails.

It's a good reminder that scams can show up in ordinary parts of life.

**Read the original article:** [AI scams aren't just tricking your parents](https://stackingpaper.substack.com/p/ai-scams-arent-just-tricking-your)

Let's put those lessons into everyday family situations.

### 1. The bank message

You receive a text claiming that your account has a suspicious charge. It tells you to click a link to stop the transaction.

The message looks professional. The bank logo is there. The wording is clean.

What should you do?

Don't start by deciding whether the message looks real. Start by asking whether you need to use that link at all.

Open your bank's official app or type its known website address yourself. You can also call the number printed on your card or on an official statement.

If there's genuinely a problem, you can address it through a channel you trust.

**The rule: Don't use the contact details in a message to verify that same message.**

### 2. The unexpected payment

Someone you don't know sends you money through a payment app. Shortly afterward, a stranger contacts you and asks you to send it back.

They sound reasonable. Maybe they say it was an honest mistake. Maybe they're panicking.

Before you send anything, stop.

The original payment could be fraudulent, and sending money back may create a separate transaction that is difficult to recover. The original payment could later be reversed, leaving you out of pocket.

Don't spend the money, and don't send a separate payment to the stranger. Contact the payment service or your bank using its official support channel and ask how to handle it.

**The rule: An unexpected payment followed by pressure to return money is a reason to pause—not rush.**

### 3. The job offer

Your child, a friend, or even you are looking for work. An email arrives about a position you actually applied for.

It uses the right company name and mentions the right role. It asks you to upload documents or provide personal information quickly to secure an interview.

AI can make a fake recruiting message sound polished. Scammers may also use information from public job listings or professional profiles to make their messages more convincing.

Before sharing anything, check the company's official careers page. Verify the sender's email domain and contact the organization through independently confirmed details if anything feels off.

Be especially cautious if the recruiter asks you to pay fees, deposit a check and send money elsewhere, or provide sensitive information before you've verified the opportunity.

**The rule: A message that knows details about your application hasn't necessarily come from the real employer.**

## What about the voice that sounds exactly like your family?

This is where the stakes can feel personal.

AI tools can be used to imitate voices, and scammers may try to make a call sound as if it's coming from someone you love.

Imagine a grandparent receives a call from someone who sounds like their grandchild. The caller says they've been in trouble and need money immediately.

Fear takes over. The grandparent wants to help.

That's exactly why families should agree on a plan *before* a frightening call ever happens.

Choose a family verification phrase or another private way to check an unusual request. If a call asks for urgent money, hang up and call the person back on a number you already know. If you can't reach them, contact another trusted family member.

Don't rely on the voice alone.

And remember: a legitimate emergency doesn't mean you must follow a stranger's instructions without checking.

**The rule: Verify the person through a separate channel before sending money or sharing sensitive information.**

## The DadCyberTips pause test

When something unexpected lands in your inbox, on your phone, or in a call, take a breath and ask these five questions:

1. **Is someone rushing me?** Urgency is often used to stop you from thinking clearly.
2. **Am I being asked to click, pay, share a code, or reveal personal information?** Treat these requests carefully, even when the message looks legitimate.
3. **Can I verify this another way?** Open the official app, use a known website, or call a number you already trust.
4. **Does the request make sense?** A bank shouldn't need you to transfer money to a “safe account.” A genuine support representative shouldn't need your password or one-time login code.
5. **Have I asked someone I trust?** A second opinion can help you notice what adrenaline is making you miss.

You don't have to solve the mystery in thirty seconds.

In fact, if someone is insisting that you must act immediately, that is a good reason to slow down.

## Make this a family habit—not just a warning

Parents, this is worth discussing with your kids before they encounter a suspicious message.

Ask them what they would do if a gaming friend asked for a login code. Talk about what to do if an online job offer requests personal information. Explain that a video, voice message, or profile picture isn't automatic proof of identity.

And for aging parents or grandparents, keep the conversation respectful. Don't make them feel embarrassed for asking for help. Agree on a simple rule: **if a message or call creates panic or demands secrecy, pause and verify it with family.**

The goal isn't to make anyone afraid of every text, phone call, or email.

The goal is to build a habit that works even when a scam looks convincing.

## If you think you've been scammed

Act quickly, but don't panic.

- Contact your bank or payment provider using its official number or app if money or financial details are involved.
- Change compromised passwords, starting with your email and any accounts that reused the same password.
- Enable multi-factor authentication where possible.
- If you shared identity information, visit the [Federal Trade Commission's IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) for recovery steps.
- Report scams and find guidance at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).
- Save relevant messages, emails, phone numbers, receipts, and screenshots if it's safe to do so.

If you sent money, don't let embarrassment delay you from asking for help. Recovery isn't always possible, but acting quickly gives your bank or service provider a chance to advise you on the available options.

## The takeaway: trust, but verify

AI is changing what scams look and sound like. But it hasn't changed the basic playbook: scammers still try to make us afraid, excited, embarrassed, or rushed enough to act without checking.

You don't need to become an AI expert to protect your family.

You need a pause. A verification habit. And an environment where everyone—from your youngest gamer to your oldest family member—feels safe saying, “Something about this doesn't seem right. Can you help me check?”

That's what online safety looks like in real life.

**No fear. No shame. Just pause, verify, and protect each other.**
